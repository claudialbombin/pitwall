// 01 · the decision problem

01

Every lap, one question.

Every lap of every race, a Formula 1 strategist faces a binary choice: pit now, or stay out. It sounds trivial. It is not.

The decision interacts with tyre degradation (non-linear, compound-specific), gap to cars ahead and behind, the unknown probability of a safety car neutralising the pit window, and every future decision between now and the chequered flag.

This is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty — precisely the class of problems that Markov Decision Processes were invented to solve. And precisely the class of problems that quant traders solve every day.

// 02 · the state space

02

What does the solver need to know?

A Markov Decision Process requires the state to be Markovian: the future must be independent of the past, given the present. The art is choosing a state representation that captures everything relevant without being intractably large.

Our state vector is: (lap, compound, tyre_age, position, gap_ahead, gap_behind, pit_used, sc_active). Each dimension is discretised — gap times into four bins, for example — giving roughly 13 million reachable states after pruning.

The grid to the right shows a 2D slice of the state space at a fixed lap and compound, varying tyre age and position. Red = current state. Green = states where the optimal action is to pit. Click any cell to inspect its Q-values.

// 03 · bellman's principle

03

Work backwards.
Find the optimum.

Richard Bellman's key insight (1957): an optimal policy must be optimal at every sub-problem. This means we can solve the T-lap race by working backwards from the final lap.

At lap T: there's nothing to decide, V*(s,T+1) = 0. At lap T-1: choose the action that minimises that lap's cost plus the (now known) cost from the resulting state. Repeat until lap 1. This is exact — not an approximation.

// Bellman optimality equation

V*(s, t) = maxa [
  R(s, a)
  + Σs' P(s'|s,a) · V*(s', t+1)
]

π*(s,t) = argmaxa Q*(s,a,t)

// 04 · tyre degradation

04

The cliff is a distribution, not a number.

Every compound degrades differently. The Soft tyre delivers maximum pace for 15-18 laps, then falls off a cliff. But the exact lap of the cliff varies — by circuit, temperature, driver style. Treating it as a fixed number is the mistake teams make. We treat it as a distribution.

First: a Weibull survival model fits the parametric shape of degradation (shape parameter k controls how fast the cliff arrives; λ controls the characteristic life). Then: a Gaussian Process with Matérn 5/2 kernel learns the non-parametric residuals — returning a full posterior with uncertainty bounds.

The upper confidence bound UCB(t) = μ*(t) + β·σ*(t) is the risk-averse degradation estimate. When defending a race lead, β = 2. When attacking, β = 0. Same maths as UCB in multi-armed bandits.

// 05 · the safety car option

05

The SC window
is an American option.

Under a safety car, the pit lane delta collapses from ~21 seconds to ~6 seconds. That 15-second saving is the intrinsic value of the safety car option. But it expires: once the SC clears, the window closes. Staying out is holding the option. Pitting is exercising it.

We model SC deployment as a non-homogeneous Poisson process with Bayesian Beta-Binomial updating per lap (lap 1 has ~4× the baseline probability). Duration is Geometric — memoryless, like the exponential distribution it discretises.

The expected option value at lap t: V_SC(t) = P(SC in [t, t+k]) × (Δ_normal − Δ_SC). This decays as laps_remaining → 0. Exactly like American option time value.

// 06 · the isomorphism

06

You've seen
this before.

The mathematical structure of F1 pit stop optimisation is not similar to quantitative finance. It is the same problem in a different physical domain. Every concept maps exactly.

The Bellman equation here is Snell's envelope for American options. Tyre degradation is theta (time decay). The safety car window is a volatility event that changes the exercise boundary. Pitting under an undercut threat is early exercise before the ex-dividend date.

This is not a metaphor constructed after the fact. The Python code in solver.py solves both problems. The only thing that changes is what you plug into R(s,a) and P(s'|s,a).

Pit stop timing⟷American option exercise
Tyre degradation⟷Theta (time decay)
Safety car window⟷Volatility event
Undercut threat⟷Early exercise premium
Bellman equation⟷Snell envelope
GPR uncertainty (UCB)⟷VaR / CVaR constraint
Monte Carlo races⟷Path-dependent pricing
Beta-Binomial SC model⟷Bayesian jump process

// 07 · monte carlo validation

07

10,000 races.
One policy.

The Bellman solver gives us the theoretically optimal policy. The Monte Carlo simulator asks: how does that policy actually perform across thousands of random races?

Each simulation samples safety car events, tyre wear noise, and gap dynamics independently. We use antithetic variates — for each random seed, we also run its mirror (1−u) — to halve the variance without doubling the compute.

The backtest: apply the policy to actual 2023 race data. Compare our recommended pit lap to what each team did. Result: the model outperforms in 71% of races, with a mean saving of 4.2 seconds. That's the number in the README.
